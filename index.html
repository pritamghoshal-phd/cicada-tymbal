The tymbal of a cicada

Nature's sound-generating metastructure, and one model that sings like many species.

In brief

Cicadas generate sound through the interplay of tymbal buckling and body resonance, but a unified framework explaining the diversity of calls across species has been absent. We conceptualize the cicada's sound-generation mechanism as a naturally occurring biological metastructure, characterized by periodically arranged ribs and inherent frequency filtering.

We introduce a mechanical model comprising a chain of bistable oscillators coupled to a chain of resonators, which captures both the snap-through dynamics of the tymbals and the subsequent acoustic filtering. It is the first species-adaptable framework capable of reproducing the distinct song frequencies and call patterns observed across multiple cicada species.

What we found

The ribs snap one after another

Filming the dog-day cicada, Neotibicen canicularis, at 39,700 frames per second shows the tymbal buckling as a traveling wave. It starts at the posterior rib and moves forward. Once it reaches the anterior end, the muscles relax and an unbuckling wave races back, far faster than the forward wave. Each snap is a click; many of these rebound sequences together make the song.

  • 39,700 frames per second of high-speed video
  • ~3 ms for the forward buckling wave
  • ~0.2 ms for the reflected unbuckling wave

The body works as a frequency filter

Vibrations measured on the tymbal itself, by high-speed imaging and laser vibrometry, span roughly 500 Hz to 15 kHz. The sound that leaves the insect does not. The abdominal air cavity amplifies a band around 3 to 5.5 kHz and suppresses frequencies below about 2 kHz, opening a band gap. That selective filtering is a hallmark of metamaterials.

  • ~3.8 kHz predicted resonance of the abdominal cavity
  • < 2 kHz band gap in the recorded call

A chain of snapping trusses coupled to resonators

We represent the tymbal as a chain of bistable von Mises trusses with viscoelastic coupling, and the body as a chain of linear spring–mass resonators. In the continuum limit, the tymbal displacement \(U\) and resonator displacement \(V\) obey

\[\frac{\partial^2 U}{\partial T^2}+\zeta\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}+U^3-3U^2+(\lambda_1+\lambda_2+2)U-R_1\frac{\partial^2 U}{\partial X^2}-\lambda_2 V=0\]
\[\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial T^2}+M\lambda_2 V-M\lambda_2 U-R_2\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial X^2}=0\]

Six nondimensional parameters \((R_1, R_2, \lambda_1, \lambda_2, M, \zeta)\) set the behaviour. The model reproduces the soliton-like buckling front, the fast reflected unbuckling wave, and two frequency bands separated by a band gap, matching the experiments.

One model, many species

From a dataset of 60 species, we tuned the model to five more with very different songs, from the bladder cicada Cystosoma saundersii to Magicicada cassini. Adjusting the parameters and the muscle actuation reproduces each species' frequency bands and call patterns, including chirps and trills.

Because each parameter maps to anatomy, such as cavity volume or tymbal stiffness, the model points toward an inverse problem: inferring body properties from a recording, and one day perhaps estimating the songs of extinct species from fossils.

Why it matters beyond cicadas

The tymbal turns modest muscle input into millisecond-scale, large-deformation snaps and then tunes the result acoustically. Those are exactly the properties sought in soft robotic actuators, shape-morphing surfaces and programmable metamaterials. The coupled bistable–resonator chain also suggests routes to band-gap tuning, targeted energy transfer, energy harvesting, and structural health monitoring with physical reservoir computing.

How we looked

Micro-CT

X-ray tomography at 43.2 µm per voxel revealed the tymbal muscles, apodeme and air cavity in 3D.

High-speed imaging

A Phantom camera at 39,700 fps tracked the in-plane motion of three tymbal ribs.

Laser vibrometry

Out-of-plane tymbal velocity sampled at 204.8 kHz alongside simultaneous audio.

Material testing

Creep tests on excised tymbals, fitted to a Prony series, gave the viscoelastic properties.
