The tymbal of a cicada

How a row of snapping ribs and a hollow belly make a cicada sing, and one simple model that can sing like many species.

How a cicada sings

On each side of a male cicada's abdomen sits a tymbal: a small, stiff, ribbed patch of cuticle. It is a drum that the cicada plays from the inside, and its song comes from two simple steps.

  1. 1The ribs pop, like a jar lid

    Press the dome on a jar lid and it gives way with a click, then pops back when you let go. Each rib in the tymbal does the same thing: it has two stable shapes and snaps between them. A muscle inside the body tugs the tymbal inward and the ribs buckle one after another, rear to front, like a row of dominoes. When the muscle lets go they spring back, much faster. Every snap is a click, a sharp burst that contains a smear of many frequencies at once.

  2. 2The body picks the pitch, like a bottle

    Blow across the mouth of a bottle and it hums one note, set by how much air is inside. A cicada's abdomen is largely hollow, and the air inside does the same job. It boosts a narrow range of pitches from the clicks and mutes the rest. What comes out is no longer a noisy click but a tuned song, and because body sizes and shapes differ, each species sounds different.

What is a metamaterial?

Most materials get their properties from what they are made of. A metamaterial gets them from how it is built: a small unit repeated over and over, so the pattern, not the substance, decides how waves pass through it. Many are designed to block some frequencies completely while letting others through, a gap engineers call a band gap.

The tymbal fits that description: a row of near-identical snapping ribs joined to a body that filters out the low notes. Engineers have only been building metamaterials on purpose for a few decades. Cicadas have been carrying one around for millions of years.

Published abstract

Cicadas generate sound through the interplay of tymbal buckling and body resonance, but a unified framework explaining the diversity of calls across species has been absent. In this study, we conceptualize the cicada's sound-generation mechanism as a naturally occurring biological metastructure, characterized by periodically arranged ribs and inherent frequency filtering. We introduce a novel mechanical model comprising a chain of bistable oscillators coupled to a chain of resonators, which successfully captures both the snap-through dynamics of the tymbals and the subsequent acoustic filtering. This model represents the first species-adaptable framework capable of reproducing the distinct song frequencies and call patterns observed across multiple cicada species, offering a new understanding of this remarkable biomechanical system.

What we found

The ribs really do snap one after another

We filmed the dog-day cicada, Neotibicen canicularis, with a camera shooting almost 40,000 frames a second. The tymbal does not buckle all at once. The buckling starts at the rear rib and moves forward, rib by rib. When it reaches the front, the muscle relaxes and the ribs pop back more than ten times faster than they went in. Each snap is a click, and a cicada's song is many of these in-and-out sequences repeated in quick succession.

  • 39,700 frames per second of high-speed video
  • ~3 ms for the ribs to buckle front to back
  • ~0.2 ms for them to pop back

The body throws away the notes it doesn't want

On the tymbal itself, the vibrations cover a huge range, from about 500 Hz (around the B above middle C) up to 15 kHz, close to the top of human hearing. The sound that actually leaves the insect is much narrower. The air in the abdomen boosts pitches between roughly 3 and 5.5 kHz and almost silences everything below about 2 kHz. That missing stretch of frequencies is a band gap, the signature of a metamaterial.

  • ~3.8 kHz predicted natural pitch of the air in the abdomen
  • < 2 kHz nearly silent in the recorded call

A model built from snapping links and springs

To capture both steps at once, we model the tymbal as a row of tiny snapping links, each with two resting positions like the jar lid, joined to their neighbours by soft springs. Underneath sits a second row of masses on springs that stands in for the body and rings at its own pitch. Kick the first link and the model does what the real insect does: a snap travels down the row, a faster snap comes back, and the body turns the clicks into two bands of sound with a quiet gap between them.

For the curious: the equations

Each snapping link is a bistable von Mises truss with viscoelastic coupling to its neighbours, and the body is a chain of linear spring–mass resonators. Treating the chain as continuous, the tymbal displacement \(U\) and the resonator displacement \(V\) obey

\[\frac{\partial^2 U}{\partial T^2}+\zeta\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}+U^3-3U^2+(\lambda_1+\lambda_2+2)U-R_1\frac{\partial^2 U}{\partial X^2}-\lambda_2 V=0\]
\[\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial T^2}+M\lambda_2 V-M\lambda_2 U-R_2\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial X^2}=0\]

The \(U^3-3U^2\) terms give each rib its two stable shapes. \(R_1\) and \(R_2\) set how strongly neighbours pull on each other along the tymbal and along the body, \(\lambda_1\) is the truss stiffness, \(\lambda_2\) couples tymbal to body, \(M\) sets the body's resonance and \(\zeta\) is damping. These six nondimensional numbers are enough to reproduce the soliton-like buckling front, the fast reflected unbuckling wave, and two frequency bands separated by a band gap. One model, many voices

Cicada songs vary enormously, from buzzes to chirps to trills. Using a dataset of 60 species, we retuned the same model for five more with very different songs, from Australia's bladder cicada, Cystosoma saundersii, to the North American periodical cicada Magicicada cassini. Changing a few settings and the rhythm of the muscle was enough to match each species' pitch and call pattern.

Each setting corresponds to something physical, such as the size of the air cavity or the stiffness of the tymbal. That opens the door to running the model in reverse: listening to a recording and working out what the insect's body must be like. One day it might even let us estimate how extinct cicadas sounded, from their fossils.

Beyond the paper · work in progress

Pulling the trigger: the muscle

The model in the paper describes the tymbal and the body. It starts each click with a single kick to the first rib. A real cicada does something different: a muscle pulls steadily on the tymbal, then lets go.

Picture a rib's two shapes as two valleys with a hill between them, and the rib as a ball resting in the first valley. Pulling on the rib tilts the whole landscape. Pull a little and the ball just shifts. Pull harder and, at a tipping point, the first valley flattens out and vanishes. The ball has nowhere to go but over the hill: that is the snap.

When the muscle lets go, the landscape tilts back, and what happens next depends on the rib. If the second valley is still there with no pull at all, the ball stays in it, like a jar lid stuck inside out. If that valley only exists while the rib is being pulled, the ball rolls home by itself and the rib snaps back. Real tymbals spring back when the muscle relaxes, which points to the second kind of rib. Try both below.

For the curious: adding the muscle to the equations

The tymbal equation above has no forcing; the paper sets the motion going with an initial displacement of the first rib. The muscle can enter as a force on the right-hand side,

\[\frac{\partial^2 U}{\partial T^2}+\zeta\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}+U^3-3U^2+(\lambda_1+\lambda_2+2)U-R_1\frac{\partial^2 U}{\partial X^2}-\lambda_2 V=F(T)\,g(X)\]

where \(F(T)\) is the muscle's pull over one twitch (ramp up, hold, release) and \(g(X)\) describes how the apodeme's pull spreads along the membrane, for example \(g(X)=e^{-X/\ell}\), largest at the rear of the tymbal where the muscle attaches.

For a slow pull on one rib, with the body keeping up (\(V=U\)), the rib sits where the force balances: \(F = U^3-3U^2+(\lambda_1+2)U\). That curve has a peak and a dip at

\[U_\pm = 1 \pm \sqrt{1-\frac{\lambda_1+2}{3}}\]

The rib snaps in when \(F\) passes the peak, \(F_\text{in}=F(U_-)\), and snaps back when \(F\) falls below the dip, \(F_\text{back}=F(U_+)\). That gives three regimes:

  • \(\lambda_1 < 0.25\): \(F_\text{back} < 0\). The buckled shape survives release; something must push the rib back.
  • \(0.25 < \lambda_1 < 1\): \(0 < F_\text{back} < F_\text{in}\). Pull to snap, release to snap back, as real tymbals do.
  • \(\lambda_1 > 1\): no peak or dip, so the rib bends smoothly and never snaps.

Next steps we are exploring: treating the muscle and apodeme as a spring in series with the rib (which decides whether the muscle acts like a force or a position control, and how much energy the snap stores and releases), a muscle force that drops when the rib moves faster than the muscle can shorten, and trains of twitches at the firing rate to build up whole calls.

Why it matters beyond cicadas

A cicada turns a modest muscle tug into fast, large snaps, then tunes the result into a loud, clear note. Engineers want exactly that trick for soft robots, surfaces that change shape on command, and materials that can be programmed to block or pass chosen vibrations. The same snap-plus-resonator design also points to ways of tuning band gaps, steering vibration energy where it is wanted, harvesting energy from motion, and monitoring the health of structures.

How we looked

3D X-ray scans

Micro-CT at 43.2 µm per voxel revealed the tymbal muscles, the tendon-like apodeme and the air cavity inside the abdomen.

High-speed video

A camera running at 39,700 frames per second tracked three tymbal ribs as they snapped.

Laser vibrometry

A laser measured how fast the tymbal surface moved, 204,800 times a second, while a microphone recorded the sound.

Material testing

Holding removed tymbals under a steady load and watching them slowly give showed how springy, and how squishy, the material is.
